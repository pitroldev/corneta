## Is it allowed?

Yes. Twitch allows simulcasting for anyone without an individual exclusivity
agreement, and YouTube has its own guidance for streaming across platforms.
Twitch requires that its experience not be worse than on the other platforms and
forbids using your Twitch stream to send the audience to watch the competing
stream.

If you signed an individual agreement, it overrides any public guide. Read the
document or contact the platform's support before you go live.

## Get both accounts ready

On YouTube, the channel needs to be verified and have live streaming enabled.
YouTube itself says the first activation can take at least 24 hours, so don't
leave this until it's time to hit **GO LIVE**.

On Twitch, grab the current stream key from your dashboard. Don't put either key
in OBS: OBS will send a local signal to Corneta.

## Set up Twitch and YouTube

1. In Corneta, open **Platforms → Add platform**.
2. Add Twitch, paste the stream key and use **Test the server**.
3. Add YouTube. If the account is connected, Corneta can create the broadcast
   when you hit **GO LIVE**; otherwise, enter the event details.
4. Confirm that both destinations show as **Ready**.

## Check upload and quality

With local distribution, the upload you need is the sum of both outputs. One
destination at 6 Mbps and another at 4 Mbps need 10 Mbps of bitrate alone; you
still need headroom for the connection to fluctuate. YouTube recommends testing
with 1.5 to 2 times the sum as a stability reference.

<ContentImage baseName="corneta-quality-modes-en" />

If the same signal works for both, **Straight up** avoids a second re-encode. If
only one destination needs to change, **Smart** adapts just that one.

## Do a rehearsal that counts

Test with the game open, plus camera, audio, alerts and a scene with movement.
Open both viewing pages on another device and confirm video and sound. A speed
test alone doesn't reproduce the work of a live stream.

## During the stream

- keep equivalent quality on Twitch;
- don't add a QR code, banner or chat command sending the Twitch audience to the
  competing stream;
- monitor bitrate and reconnections per destination;
- if one platform drops, try reconnecting just that one before ending
  everything.

<Callout label="When the cloud makes more sense" tone="limit">
  If your upload can't handle both local outputs, a cloud relay receives a
  single signal and replicates it from there. The trade-off is putting an
  external service in the middle.
</Callout>

Use the [upload calculator](/en/guides/quality/multistream-upload-speed) before
choosing your bitrates.
