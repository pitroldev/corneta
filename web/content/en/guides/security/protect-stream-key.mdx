## The key authorizes the stream

A stream key isn't your login password, but it deserves the same care. Twitch defines the key as the authorization that identifies your channel at the ingest. On YouTube, it links the encoder to the stream you chose.

<ContentImage baseName="corneta-platform-key-setup-en" />

Anyone who gets that key can try to send video to your channel without knowing your password. That's why it never goes into a screenshot, a pasted log, a settings backup or a support ticket.

## RTMP and RTMPS

RTMP carries the signal to the ingest. RTMPS adds TLS along that path. YouTube recommends RTMPS because the upload stays encrypted all the way to the platform's infrastructure. When the dashboard offers both, prefer `rtmps://`.

RTMPS doesn't fix a key that has already leaked. It protects the transport; it doesn't stop someone who copied the credential from using it.

## If the key showed up on screen

1. End the suspicious stream.
2. Reset the key in the platform's dashboard.
3. Update the key in Corneta.
4. Review the VOD, clip, screenshot and local recording where it appeared.
5. Change your account password if there's any sign of a break-in, and turn on 2FA.

<Callout label="Blur is not revocation" tone="important">
  If a real key showed up in a file, treat it as exposed even if you blur it
  afterward. Reset first; edit the image later.
</Callout>

To know which field to copy without mixing up the addresses, see [stream key, ingest URL and the OBS key](/en/help/platforms/streaming-keys-and-urls).
